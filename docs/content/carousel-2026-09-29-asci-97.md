# Carousel — "97.3% didn't pass"

**Status:** copy only, ready for design. Not designed slides.
**Source:** ASCI Annual Complaints Report 2025–26, published 28 May 2026.
**Format:** 8 slides, Instagram square. Also works on LinkedIn.

---

## Why this one

It is the most current hard regulatory number on influencer disclosure in
India, and it points at exactly what Guapd sells: a record of what was agreed.
Nothing in it depends on our own data, so it can run before the product has
volume — and unlike a vendor's fraud report, the source is a regulator with no
product to sell.

**The care it needs:** this is a number about ads ASCI *reviewed*, not about
every influencer ad in India. The sample reaches ASCI through complaints and
monitoring, so it is weighted toward ads someone already thought were wrong.
Every slide below says "reviewed" or "checked" for that reason. Written as
"97.3% of influencer ads break the rules" it is a claim a journalist can take
apart in one phone call, and it would deserve to be.

## Caption

> India's advertising regulator reviewed 1,609 influencer ads in FY26. 97.3% of
> them required modification.
>
> Worth being precise about what that means: these are ads ASCI looked at, not
> a random sample of the whole market. But the pattern inside it is the
> interesting part. 54% involved legally restricted categories, mostly offshore
> betting. The rest were ordinary brand deals — and they still didn't pass.
>
> Most of it wasn't fraud. It was missing disclosures, vague terms, and no
> record of what either side had actually agreed to.
>
> That's not a talent problem or an honesty problem. It's what happens when the
> deal lives in a chat thread.
>
> (ASCI Annual Complaints Report 2025–26, published May 2026.)

## Do not claim

Carried from the brief, plus one added here.

- **Do not say this happened "this week" or "just now."** The report published
  28 May 2026. It is the most current figure, not a new one. The caption dates
  it on purpose.
- **Do not imply 97.3% means fraud.** ASCI attributes most violations to
  disclosure and restricted-category issues, not fake engagement or fake
  followers. Slide 5 says this explicitly and should not be cut for length.
- **Do not name the brands in the ASCI report.** Those violations belong to
  those companies' campaigns, not to an industry-wide claim, and naming them
  turns a category argument into an accusation.
- **ADDED: do not drop the word "reviewed."** "97.3% of influencer ads failed"
  is a different and wrong claim — it turns a regulator's complaint-weighted
  caseload into a market-wide rate. Every slide here is written to survive
  someone checking it.
